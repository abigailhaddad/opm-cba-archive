VERNON D. OLIVER, United States District Judge:

         On March 27, 2025, President Donald Trump signed Executive Order 14,251 (“EO

14,251”), which excluded the Department of Justice (“DOJ”) and its member agencies,

including the Federal Bureau of Prisons (“BOP”) from their previously statutorily protected

right to collective bargaining. Nevertheless, for nearly six months after the issuance of

EO 14,251, BOP continued to uphold and substantially comply with the collective bargaining

agreement (the “CBA”) it held with National Council of Prison Locals, American Federation

of Government Employees (“CPL-33”). This arrangement continued until September 25,

2026, when BOP Director William K. Marshall III chose to terminate the CBA for a reason

unrelated to national security: because, as he stated, CPL-33 is not the “kind of union” he

supports. Although BOP stated that the termination was based on EO 14,251,1 Director

Marshall posted a Message from the Director on the BOP website that same day, claiming that

I.        BACKGROUND

          A.     Factual Background

                 1.     The Parties

          CPL-33 represents a bargaining unit of approximately 30,000 civil servants who work

for BOP.3 Its members are correctional officers, case managers, psychologists, and other BOP

employees who are responsible for maintaining the safety and security of inmates, staff, and

BOP institutions.4 CPL-33 evolved from labor organizations that had represented federal

correctional institution employees since the 1940s.5 Under its current name, CPL-33 has been

the exclusive bargaining representative for BOP employees since 2006.6

          CPL-33’s mission is to advocate for and promote the interests of its bargaining unit

members in their federal employment.7 The union’s core functions are: (1) collectively

bargaining with BOP to obtain a fair and reasonable collective bargaining agreement; (2) filing

and negotiating grievances against BOP to enforce the terms of the CBA; (3) pursuing

arbitration on behalf of its members to enforce the CBA; (4) providing trainings to constituent

locals, like Local 1661; and (5) providing support, guidance, and resources to its members.8

CPL-33 is also active in advocating for its members before Congress to seek greater safety

measures for staff and inmates, improved working conditions, and increased staffing and pay.9

II.      LEGAL STANDARD

         A.     Preliminary Injunction

         A preliminary injunction is “an extraordinary remedy that may only be awarded upon

a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such relief.” Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council,

Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 22 (2008). A party seeking a preliminary injunction that “will affect

government action taken in the public interest pursuant to statute or a regulatory scheme” must

demonstrate: (1) irreparable harm absent injunctive relief, (2) a likelihood of success on the

merits, (3) public interest weighing in favor of granting the injunction, and (4) that the balance

of equities tips in its favor. We The Patriots USA, Inc. v. Hochul, 17 F.4th 266, 279–80 (2d

Cir. 2021) (quoting Agudath Israel of Am. v. Cuomo, 983 F.3d 620, 631 (2d Cir. 2020)),

opinion clarified, 17 F.4th 368 (2d Cir. 2021); see also Yang v. Kosinski, 960 F.3d 119, 127

(2d Cir. 2020). When the government is a party to the suit, the balance of the equities and

public interest factors of the preliminary injunction standard merge. New York v. U.S. Dep’t of

        A movant seeking injunctive relief has a “heavier burden” than a plaintiff who must

        B.     5 U.S.C. § 705

        The APA authorizes courts to “issue all necessary and appropriate process to postpone

the effective date of an agency action or to preserve status or rights pending conclusion of the

review proceedings” when “necessary to prevent irreparable injury.” 5 U.S.C. § 705. “The

standard for a stay of agency action under 5 U.S.C. § 705 is the same as the standard for a

preliminary injunction.” New York v. Admin. for Child. & Fams., 833 F. Supp. 3d 361, 381

(S.D.N.Y. 2026).

               2.      Administrative Procedures Act

       The Court declined to adjudicate the merits of the APA claim in the first instance in

deciding the Motion to Dismiss, finding that, “[t]he Court cannot consider Defendants’ fact-

specific arguments on the current record, without the benefit of the certified administrative

record or the additional breadth of evidence typically available during a preliminary

injunction.” Nat. Council of Prison Locs., 2026 WL 1146301, at *9. Now, with the benefit of

the affidavits and the factual record before the Court on the PI Motion, the Court will address

the likely merit of Plaintiffs’ APA claim.

       Arbitrary-and-capricious review under the APA requires the Court to “ensure[] that the

agency has acted within a zone of reasonableness and, in particular, has reasonably considered

the relevant issues and reasonably explained the decision.” FCC v. Prometheus Radio Project,

592 U.S. 414, 423 (2021). “Not only must an agency’s decreed result be within the scope of

its lawful authority, but the process by which it reaches that result must be logical and rational.”

Michigan v. EPA, 576 U.S. 743, 750 (2015) (quoting Allentown Mack Sales & Serv. Inc. v.

NLRB, 522 U.S. 359, 374 (1998)). “An agency action is arbitrary and capricious if the agency

has relied on factors which Congress has not intended it to consider, entirely failed to consider

an important aspect of the problem, offered an explanation for its decision that runs counter to

the evidence before the agency, or is so implausible that it could not be ascribed to a difference

in view or the product of agency expertise.” Kakar v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 29

F.4th 129, 132 (2d Cir. 2022) (quoting Alzokari v. Pompeo, 973 F.3d 65, 70 (2d Cir. 2020)).

Agency action is also arbitrary and capricious where the agency fails to consider “serious

reliance interests” endangered by the change of a longstanding policy. FDA v. Wages & White

Lion Invs., L.L.C., 604 U.S. 542, 568–69 (2025) (quoting Encino Motorcars, LLC v. Navarro,

579 U.S. 211, 221–22 (2016)).

          The APA carries a “strong presumption that Congress intends judicial review of

administrative action.” County of Westchester v. HUD, 778 F.3d 412, 418–19 (2d Cir. 2015)

(quoting Conyers v. Rossides, 558 F.3d 137, 143 (2d Cir. 2009)). “The presumption in favor

of judicial review is not absolute, but subject to a narrow exception: the APA’s prohibition

against judicial review of agency action ‘committed to agency discretion by law.’” Salazar v.

King, 822 F.3d 61, 76 (2d Cir. 2016) (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)). “To honor the

presumption of review, [the Supreme Court has] read the exception in § 701(a)(2) quite

narrowly, confining it to those rare administrative decisions traditionally left to agency

discretion.” Dep’t of Homeland Sec. v. Regents of the Univ. of Cal., 591 U.S. 1, 17 (2020)

(cleaned up). Instances of decisions “committed to agency discretion” have included “a

decision not to institute enforcement proceedings, or a decision by an intelligence agency to

terminate an employee in the interest of national security.” Dep’t of Com. v. New York, 588

U.S. 752, 772 (2019). (cleaned up). “In the absence of an express statutory prohibition, the

agency ‘bears the heavy burden of overcoming the strong presumption that Congress did not

mean to prohibit all judicial review of its decision.’” Salazar, 822 F.3d at 75 (quoting Dunlop

v. Bachowski, 421 U.S. 560, 567 (1975)).

action solely committed to an agency’s discretion by law under § 701(a)(2). First, Defendants

enforcement proceedings, see Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985), or terminated

matter is akin to the Supreme Court’s decision in Department of Homeland Security v. Regents

of the University of California, 591 U.S. 1 (2020), where the Court found that an agency’s

decision to eliminate a program offering benefits is not one that is committed to agency

discretion by law. Id. at 19. Indeed, the recission of such a program “is an action that provides

a focus for judicial review,” and ensuring “access to these types of benefits is an interest courts

often are called upon to protect.” Id. at 18–19 (cleaned up). Here too, ensuring employment

agreements are upheld and employees retain access to their agreed-upon benefits are interests

courts are often called upon to protect.

adjudicating this matter under the APA. Indeed, the District of Rhode Island did not find a

shortage of law to apply in deciding a matter with nearly identical facts under the APA, see

District Court’s decision to grant preliminary injunction). As in those cases, the Court sees no

indication that there is “no law to apply” in deciding this matter. Accordingly, Defendants’

argument fails because they did not meet the heavy burden of demonstrating that the

                           c.   Contrived and Pretextual

          The Court next turns to the first of Plaintiffs’ six arguments: that Defendants’ decision

to terminate the CBA was “contrived or pretextual” in violation of the APA because its

explanation did not “accurately reflect the reason the agency took the action.”100 As the

Supreme Court explained in Department of Commerce v. New York, “a court may not reject an

agency’s stated reasons for acting simply because the agency might also have had other

unstated reasons,” but an agency’s stated reason for its action must not be “incongruent with

what the record reveals about the agency’s priorities and decisionmaking process.” 588 U.S.

752, 781, 785 (2019).

          On September 25, 2025, Defendants published two incongruent explanations for the

termination of the CBA. In the termination letter sent to CPL-33 President White, BOP cited

the Executive Order as the basis for the termination, stating that the termination was “based

on the EO.”101 But that same day, Director Marshall posted his message that heavily criticized

CPL-33’s operations and stated that the “whole purpose of ending this contract” was because

the “union [was] an obstacle to progress” and “the current contract has too often slowed or

prevented changes.”102 The letter did not state that EO 14,251 required the termination of the

CBA. Nor did it mention the national security rationale underlying the Executive Order.

Instead, it only cited to Defendants’ criticisms of the union. Although an agency may have

multiple reasons for its action, it cannot offer an entirely pretextual explanation that is

undermined by its own contradictory assertions made the same day. But that is precisely what
